## Entry 001 — Unemployment Insurance Denial: Appeal Preparation Gap

**Who:** A warehouse worker in Indiana just received a notice saying her UI claim was denied for "misconduct." She has 14 days to file an appeal. She doesn't know what misconduct means legally, what evidence to gather, or what she'll need to say at the phone hearing in 4–6 weeks. She earns $16/hr. She cannot afford an employment attorney.

**When:** The 10–14 day window after receiving an initial denial. This is where preparation begins. Hearings follow 4–10 weeks later.

**The pattern:**
- 65% of initial SSDI/disability applications are denied. UI denials are similarly high — many states report 40–50%+ initial denial rates for contested separations.
- Hearings have well-defined denial reason codes (Voluntary Quit, Misconduct, Availability, Ability to Work, etc.)
- Each denial reason requires a different legal defense strategy (e.g., misconduct requires showing the behavior was a reasonable response to circumstances; voluntary quit requires showing "good cause")
- Most people who lose hearings lose them because they don't understand the legal standard they're defending against — not because they lacked evidence
- Claimyr article (2025): "Getting flustered when put on the spot" despite having solid documentation is the most common failure. "Volume over strategy" — gathering documents without a clear narrative framework

**What they've tried:**
- State unemployment website (bureaucratic, process-focused, not strategy-focused)
- Law firm pages (mostly upselling legal representation, which starts at $500–1,000 for hearing prep)
- State-provided handbooks (Michigan's is 40 pages; useful but not filterable by denial reason)
- YouTube (general advice, not denial-reason-specific)
- NY State provides a video but it's general preparation, not specific to denial type

**Specific gap:** No tool maps your denial reason → specific legal standard → preparation checklist + statement guidance. The hearing is not a narrative opportunity ("let me explain what happened") — it's a legal standard test ("does my situation meet the definition of 'good cause' under state law?"). Most people don't know this.

**Confidence: 3.5/5** — pain is real and documented, the gap is plausible, but I have limited direct forum evidence of people failing because of this specific preparation gap (vs failing for other reasons). Need more.

**NEW angle (if built):** Denial reason → legal standard explained in plain English → specific evidence checklist for that standard → statement guidance for that standard. No incumbent does this as an interactive, free, immediately-usable tool.

## Entry 002 — Elder Care Navigation: Medicare vs. Medicaid Confusion

**Who:** A 52-year-old accountant in Ohio whose father just had a stroke. The hospital discharge planner says he'll need "skilled nursing care" and may need a nursing home long-term. The accountant doesn't understand the difference between Medicare and Medicaid coverage, when one ends and the other begins, what his father's assets are protected, and what the family needs to do right now vs. over the next few months.

**When:** The immediate post-hospitalization window when a family member is transitioning from acute care to long-term care. Decisions made in this 2–4 week window have major financial consequences.

**The pattern:**
- 60% of nursing home residents have Medicaid as primary payer (KFF 2024)
- 40% of Americans incorrectly believe Medicare is the primary source of coverage for nursing home care (KFF poll)
- Medicare covers at most 100 days of skilled nursing care after a qualifying 3-day hospital stay. After day 100: patient pays everything.
- Medicaid covers long-term nursing home care but requires asset spend-down — complex rules about what's exempt (primary home, car, spousal allowances)
- The Medicaid "look-back period" (5 years) means asset transfers must have happened years ago to avoid penalties
- Elder law attorneys charge $200–500/hour; most families can't afford this for initial navigation

**What they've tried:**
- Medicare.gov (explains coverage tiers but doesn't guide decision-making for their specific situation)
- NOLO articles (accurate but generic — doesn't account for their state's rules or their father's specific asset situation)
- Hospital social workers (helpful but overwhelmed, 15-minute conversations)
- Family discussions (pooling confusion doesn't reduce confusion)

**Specific gap:** No tool walks a family through the specific sequence of questions for their situation: Is this a short-term rehab stay or long-term care? → Does the 3-day hospital stay qualify for Medicare SNF coverage? → When will Medicare coverage end? → Is Medicaid eligibility possible given current assets? → What documents to gather now? Most resources answer each question in isolation; nobody guides the sequence.

**Confidence: 3.5/5** — KFF polling data is solid evidence of confusion at scale. The specific navigation gap is plausible. But the "what to do right now" framing depends heavily on state, asset situation, and care needs — may be too complex for a static tool. Need to verify whether existing tools actually fail at the sequence guidance or just haven't been found by people in crisis.

**NEW angle (if built):** A guided decision tree for the post-hospitalization transition. Input: type of care needed, current Medicare situation, rough asset picture → outputs: which coverage applies when, what to do in the next 7 days, what requires an elder law attorney vs. what you can navigate yourself. Different from existing tools (eligibility calculators, general articles) because it guides the sequence, not just answers individual questions.

## Entry 003 — IEP Meeting Prep: Parent Power Imbalance

**Who:** A mother of a 7-year-old with ADHD in New Jersey. She just received a notice for an annual IEP review meeting in 5 days. The school team will have 8 people in the room; she'll be alone. She doesn't know what the goals in the draft IEP actually mean, whether they're legally adequate, what she can push back on, or what she can request that the school might not mention.

**When:** The 2–7 day window between receiving an IEP meeting notice and the meeting itself.

**The pattern:**
- New Jersey passed a law in 2025 specifically expanding advance notice requirements because parents weren't able to meaningfully participate in IEP meetings
- Schools are legally required to obtain parental consent for evaluations and placements, but structurally hold informational power (they have the assessments, the staff, the process knowledge)
- IEP goals can be vague and legally adequate simultaneously — parents can't tell the difference
- "Outnumbered" is a recurring complaint in parent communities
- 3% of students have IEPs in the US; that's approximately 7 million students with actively-involved parents

**What they've tried:**
- understood.org (comprehensive and high-quality)
- momjunky.com, care.com checklists (static PDFs)
- State-specific attorney pages
- Parent advocacy groups (helpful but requires finding them)

**Specific gap:** The existing tools (understood.org especially) are comprehensive about what questions to ask, but not specific to the meeting TYPE (initial evaluation, annual review, reevaluation, placement change) or the specific DISABILITY. The questions for an initial evaluation are different from questions for an annual review. The questions for an autism diagnosis are different from ADHD.

**Confidence: 2.5/5** — pain is real but the existing tool landscape is already fairly good (understood.org is excellent). The specific gap (meeting-type + disability-specific guidance) is plausible but I haven't confirmed understood.org doesn't do this already. Need to verify. Many free PDFs exist; my skeptic would immediately name understood.org.

## Entry 004 — Benefits Cliff Navigation (ELIMINATED)

**Finding:** Multiple sophisticated tools already exist: Atlanta Fed CLIFF Suite (state-specific, models interaction of all major benefits programs), Family Resource Simulator (the most comprehensive), benefitscliffs.org. The domain is well-served at the tool level.

**Decision:** Eliminated from consideration. The pain is real but the existing tool gap doesn't exist. The distribution problem (these tools aren't well-known) is a different problem from a tool-building gap.
