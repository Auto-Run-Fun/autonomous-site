New York Unemployment Insurance Appeal Prep

You were denied. You're filing an appeal. Here's what you actually need to know before your hearing.

The most important thing to understand

A UI hearing is a legal standard test, not a narrative review. The Administrative Law Judge isn't deciding whether you're sympathetic or whether your situation was reasonable. They're applying a statutory definition to the facts you present. Your job is to satisfy — or refute — specific legal elements.

Most people prepare by telling their story. The story may be true, reasonable, and compelling — and still fail to address the legal question being tested. Select your denial reason below to see exactly what is being tested.

If you just received your denial notice
What does appealing involve?
A phone or video hearing (45–90 min) before an Administrative Law Judge. Both sides give testimony and can present documents. The ALJ issues a written decision.
What happens if you don't appeal?
The denial becomes final. You lose all benefits for the affected period. After the deadline passes this is generally not reversible.
What is the deadline?
30 days from the date printed on your denial notice — not from when you receive it. Mail delays matter: if your notice was dated June 1 and arrived June 8, your deadline is still July 1. If you received yours more than 20 days after the notice date, file immediately.
How do you file?
Online at applynow.labor.ny.gov → "Request a Hearing." Or call 888-209-8124.

Misconduct
NY Labor Law §593(3) · Employer must prove all three elements
Your employer claims you were fired for misconduct. To sustain this denial, they must prove every part of a three-element test. Any element they can't prove means the denial doesn't hold.
Legal standard

Misconduct under NY law isn't just "doing something wrong." It requires all three of the following to be true. If any one is missing, the misconduct finding fails.

Three-part test — employer must prove ALL three
  • 1 Connected to employment The conduct occurred at work or directly affected the employment relationship. Off-duty personal conduct that has no effect on the job generally doesn't qualify.
  • 2 Willful or deliberate The employee made a conscious choice to act that way — not an accident, not an inability to perform, not a single moment of poor judgment after years of clean service. "I couldn't do it" is different from "I refused to do it."
  • 3 Disregard for employer's legitimate interests The act shows the employee doesn't care about the employer's reasonable business needs. Isolated poor performance, inability to meet standards, and good-faith disagreements generally don't meet this element.
Not misconduct: poor job performance, inability (vs. unwillingness), a single incident after a clean record, good-faith policy disagreement, medical absence without falsification.
Misconduct: falsifying records after warnings, repeated unexcused absences despite written warnings, deliberate violation of a known safety policy, harassment of coworkers.
If you were fired for a drug or alcohol test (positive result or refusal): This still runs through the same §593(3) three-element test — there is no separate drug/alcohol category in NY law. But the Appeals Board has developed specific rules you can use:
  • Written policy required. The employer must have a written drug/alcohol testing policy that you received and that explicitly states a positive test (not just "impairment") is a policy violation. Oral policies are generally insufficient.
  • First positive test alone may not equal misconduct. If the employer policy doesn't expressly link a first positive to discharge, a single test without prior warnings or documented impairment at work can fail element 2 (willful/deliberate) or element 3 (disregard for interests).
  • Refusal vs. positive result. Refusing to take a mandatory test is treated differently from testing positive — but only if the policy explicitly covers refusal as a violation. If the policy only addresses positive results, a refusal may not meet the standard.
  • Alcoholism relapse exception. Alcoholism may be treated as an illness (beyond the employee's control), not a voluntary choice — weakening element 2. This does not apply to illegal drug use. If alcoholism was a diagnosed, documented condition, raise this specifically and have medical documentation ready.

At the hearing, the employer will present their evidence. You have the right to cross-examine their witnesses. You do not have to accept their account as fact. Prepare specific questions in advance: "Did you personally witness the incident?" "Is this policy documented in the employee handbook I was given?" "Are there records of prior written warnings to me?" Cross-examination is where you challenge the employer's version of each element.
Statement guidance — what to focus on

The ALJ will ask you to tell your side. Here's how to structure it to address what they're actually evaluating.

  • →Challenge the elements, don't just tell your story. Start by naming which elements of the test you're addressing: "I want to address whether the conduct was willful and whether it showed disregard for the company's interests."
  • →If no written policy existed, name that. "I was not provided a written copy of this policy and was not trained on it" directly challenges element 1 or 2.
  • →If you weren't warned before, name that. "This was my first incident in four years. There were no prior warnings." This challenges element 3.
  • →If the act wasn't deliberate, distinguish inability from refusal. "I was unable to complete the task due to [medical condition / equipment failure / lack of training], not because I chose not to."
  • →Lead with your strongest element challenge. If you have a clean 5-year record, open with that. If the policy was unwritten, open with that. Don't bury it.
  • →Do NOT open with the narrative of what happened. Opening with "here's what occurred" doesn't address the legal standard. You'll get to the facts, but the frame should be: here's why the three-element test isn't met.
Voluntary Quit Without Good Cause
NY Labor Law §593(1) · Claimant must show good cause
You left the job. The question is whether your reason meets the legal definition of "good cause" — which is specific and different from "a reasonable reason to quit."
Legal standard

"Good cause" has a specific legal meaning under NY law. It isn't "a legitimate personal reason." It means circumstances developed in employment that would have justified refusing the job in the first place. The test has three parts.

Three-part test — claimant must show all three
  • 1 Circumstances developed in employment Something specific changed — or existed in a way you couldn't have known — about the job conditions, employer behavior, or your personal situation directly connected to the employment.
  • 2 Steps taken to address it before quitting In most cases, you must show you tried to fix the problem before leaving. An HR complaint, an accommodation request, a documented conversation with management. "I couldn't work there anymore" without prior steps usually fails.
  • 3 The reason would justify refusing the job initially The standard the hearing officer applies: would a reasonable person have refused this job from the start, knowing what you now know? If yes, leaving is protected.
Circumstances that typically meet "good cause": documented domestic violence requiring departure; health/medical necessity with substantiation; immediate family member's illness requiring care the employer won't accommodate; relocation required by a spouse's military orders; constructive discharge (employer made conditions so hostile that a reasonable person would have quit).
What typically does NOT meet "good cause": job stress (without specific documented conditions), a better opportunity elsewhere, a difficult manager (without documented attempts to resolve), voluntary lifestyle changes, feeling undervalued without a specific triggering circumstance.
Evidence to gather before your hearing
Statement guidance
  • →Name the statutory category your situation falls under. "My situation involves [health necessity with medical documentation / documented domestic violence / constructive discharge]. These circumstances meet the good cause standard because..."
  • →Show the steps you took before leaving. "Before I resigned, I sent a written accommodation request on [date]. The employer's response was [describe]. I gave them [X days] to address it."
  • →Connect your reason to "would you have taken the job knowing this?" The question the ALJ is answering: would a reasonable person have refused this position from the start? Frame your answer to that question specifically.
  • →If you have medical documentation, lead with it. "My doctor has provided documentation that I needed to leave this position due to [condition]" — then introduce the document.
  • →Do NOT say "I couldn't take it anymore" without the specific legal basis. Stress is real. It doesn't meet the legal standard. Use the statutory language: "circumstances developed in my employment that would have justified refusing the job initially."
Availability / Failure to Seek Work
NY Labor Law §591 · Claimant must satisfy all three prongs
Your claim was denied because DOL believes you weren't available for work, weren't seeking work, or both. This is a documentation problem as much as a facts problem.
Legal standard

To receive UI benefits in NY, you must continuously satisfy three separate prongs. A denial on availability or job search means one or more wasn't met.

Three-prong test — all three must be satisfied each week
  • 1 Registered for employment You must be registered at the NY Department of Labor. This is typically established when you file your initial claim.
  • 2 Available for work You must be available to accept suitable employment without restrictions that would prevent you from doing so. This doesn't mean you must accept any job — it means you must be able and willing to accept suitable work. Self-imposed restrictions (only nights, only one borough, only remote) can raise availability issues.
  • 3 Actively seeking work You must make specific, documented efforts to find employment each week. NY requires a minimum number of employer contacts per week (currently 3 for most claimants). Contacts should be with real employers for real positions — blanket applications or job board browsing without applications don't count.
If you have limitations: partial availability is evaluated differently than zero availability. A doctor's note saying "no heavy lifting" still allows you to seek and accept sedentary work. Show what you can do, not just what you can't.
Evidence to gather before your hearing
Statement guidance
  • →Address each prong directly and separately. "I was registered. I was available — my only restriction was [X] which still allowed me to accept [type of work]. I was actively seeking work — here is my log of [N] contacts per week."
  • →If your limitation is medical, define the scope of your availability. Don't say "I couldn't work." Say "I could not perform physically demanding work but was available for and seeking sedentary positions, as my doctor's letter describes."
  • →If you didn't document your job search adequately: say so, and commit. "I did not maintain the required records during this period. I understand that's required and have since done so." Honesty about the gap is better than fighting facts you can't win.
  • →Have your job search log ready to present. Specific is far better than vague. "I applied to three jobs a week but didn't write it down" is weak. Dated records with employer names are evidence.
  • →Do NOT claim availability without being able to show specific job search activity. "I was looking" without documentation will not hold up. The job search log is your evidence; prepare it before the hearing.
Refusal of Suitable Work
NY Labor Law §593(2) · Claimant must show good cause for refusal
You turned down a job offer and your benefits were cut off as a result. "I didn't want that job" isn't a legal defense — but there are specific circumstances that are.
Legal standard

Refusing a job offer normally disqualifies you from UI. But the law recognizes four specific circumstances as "good cause" for refusal.

Good cause categories — show at least one applies
  • A Wage substantially below prevailing rate The offered wage is "substantially less favorable" than the prevailing wage for that occupation in that area. Substantiality is judged against what the DOL labor market data shows, not your personal expectations.
  • B Unreasonable commute Distance or expense creates an unreasonable burden. What's "unreasonable" depends on what is customary in the area. A 2-hour commute in a rural area where no transit exists is different from a 2-hour commute in NYC with reasonable transit options.
  • C Picket line / labor organization Accepting the work would require crossing an active picket line or interfering with your membership in a labor organization.
  • D Substantially less favorable conditions Work conditions (safety, hours, benefits) are substantially less favorable than prevailing local standards for comparable work.
Not good cause: personal preference, "I was looking for something better," wage that was lower than your last job (but not below the prevailing rate), commute that was inconvenient but not unreasonable.
Evidence to gather before your hearing
Statement guidance
  • →Identify which specific good-cause category applies. "My refusal was based on Category A — the offered wage of $X was substantially below the prevailing rate for this occupation in this county, which is $Y per the DOL data I've attached."
  • →Present the comparison data, not the conclusion. Don't say "the wage was too low." Say "the offered wage was $[X]. The DOL wage survey for [occupation] in [county] shows the median wage is $[Y]. The offer was [Z]% below prevailing rate."
  • →For the commute argument, show the cost calculation. "The commute was [distance / time] by the only available route. The transit cost of $[X] per day represented [Y]% of the gross daily wage. That commute cost would have left me with $[Z] per day in net pay."
  • →Do NOT lead with your personal reasons. "I didn't feel the job was right for me" is not in any of the four categories. The ALJ will ask what category you're claiming — have a category and evidence ready before you start.
Ability to Work (Medical / Other Limitation)
NY Labor Law §591(2) · Claimant must show availability for suitable work
Your claim was denied because DOL determined you were unable to work. The question is whether you could perform some category of suitable work — even with limitations.
Legal standard

Under §591(2), you are eligible for benefits when you are "available for and able and willing to accept employment." A medical limitation doesn't automatically disqualify you — the question is whether you remain available for some suitable work.

What the hearing officer will assess
  • 1 Nature and scope of the limitation What specifically can you not do? A back injury that prevents heavy lifting is different from a condition that prevents all work. Specificity matters — "I can't work" is not the same as "I can't perform jobs requiring standing for more than 2 hours."
  • 2 Whether suitable work exists within those limitations If sedentary office work, phone work, or remote work exists in your labor market and you're physically able to perform it, you likely qualify for benefits — even with a physical limitation.
  • 3 Whether you were seeking work within your limitations Were you applying to jobs you could actually do given your condition? Or to jobs that required physical capabilities you'd lost?
Partial availability is better than none. If you can do sedentary work, clerical work, phone work, or remote work — document that clearly. Complete unavailability is much harder to argue. Define what you can do, not only what you can't.
Be honest about total inability. If you genuinely cannot perform any work, the hearing officer will likely find you ineligible for that period. Do not claim availability you don't have — it can be treated as a misrepresentation.
Evidence to gather before your hearing
Statement guidance
  • →Define your availability in terms of what you can do. "While I was unable to perform physically demanding work, I was available for sedentary work, phone-based customer service, data entry, and remote administrative positions."
  • →Lead with the doctor's letter. "My treating physician has provided a letter describing my functional limitations and confirming I was able to perform sedentary work during this period." Introduce it early.
  • →Present your job search log. Show specific applications to positions within your limitations. This directly addresses the "seeking work" prong.
  • →Connect the limitation to suitable work categories. The question isn't whether you could do your old job — it's whether you could do some suitable work. Make the argument that suitable alternative work exists and you were pursuing it.
  • →Do NOT overstate your limitations. If a later employer hired you for a job you claimed you couldn't do, that will come up. Be precise about what you could and couldn't do and when.
Dismissal / Separation Pay
NY Labor Law §591(3-a) · Disqualification during the "dismissal period"
You received severance, a buyout, or separation pay when you left, and your UI benefits were denied or reduced. The question is whether that payment legally qualifies as "dismissal pay," how the weekly amount was calculated, and whether the calculation was done correctly.
Legal standard

NY law says no benefits are payable for any week in which a claimant receives "dismissal pay" that exceeds the weekly benefit rate plus the partial benefit credit. There are three things you can challenge.

Three-part challenge framework
  • 1 Was it really "dismissal pay"? The law has a narrow definition. Dismissal pay = one or more payments due to separation. Excluded from the definition: accrued vacation or sick leave, pension/retirement payments, health insurance, supplemental unemployment benefits, and WARN Act payments. If your payment falls in an excluded category, the disqualification doesn't apply.
  • 2 Did the weekly amount actually exceed the threshold? A lump sum is allocated weekly from your last day of employment. The allocation is based on your actual weekly earnings rate. Only weeks where the allocated amount exceeds your weekly benefit rate + partial benefit credit are disqualified. Lower-amount payments may not disqualify you at all — just reduce your benefit for those weeks.
  • 3 Was the allocation calculated on the right earnings rate? The DOL uses your actual weekly remuneration from your employer. If DOL used the wrong wage figure — or used the quarterly average instead of actual weekly wages — the allocation (and the period of disqualification) may be longer than it should be.
What is the partial benefit credit (PBC)? Your PBC is typically 1/4 of your weekly benefit rate (WBR). Example: WBR = $500, PBC = $125, so the disqualification threshold is $625/week. Only weeks where the allocated dismissal pay exceeds this combined amount are fully disqualified — weeks where the allocation is below the threshold may only reduce your benefit rather than eliminate it. Your WBR and PBC appear on your monetary determination letter from DOL.
Excluded payments that are NOT dismissal pay: Accrued vacation or sick leave you earned during employment. Pension or retirement benefits. Health or medical insurance payments. WARN Act payments (federally mandated notice-period pay). Supplemental unemployment benefits. If your payment is one of these, the disqualification shouldn't apply.
What IS dismissal pay (and will disqualify you for allocated weeks): Severance paid specifically because you left the company. A buyout package paid due to layoff. "Separation agreements" with ongoing payment tied to departure. Retention bonuses that pay out at separation.
Evidence to gather before your hearing
If the dismissal period has already run out: The disqualification is time-limited — it ends when the allocated dismissal pay runs out. If you're appealing a denial that covered only part of your claim period, establish the date the dismissal period should have ended. Benefits should be payable after that date even if the appeal covers earlier disqualified weeks.
Statement guidance
  • →Name which element you're challenging. "My challenge is that the payment was not 'dismissal pay' — it was [accrued vacation / WARN Act payment / pension disbursement]" is more focused than "my severance shouldn't count."
  • →If challenging the calculation: bring the numbers. "DOL calculated a weekly allocation of $[X] using an earnings rate of $[Y]/week. My actual weekly earnings were $[Z]/week as shown on my final pay stub. Using the correct rate, my dismissal period should have ended [N] weeks earlier."
  • →If challenging the category: read the language of your agreement aloud. "My separation agreement states the payment is for 'accrued paid time off.' Accrued leave is explicitly excluded from the definition of dismissal pay under §591(3-a)."
  • →If the dismissal period has ended: establish the cutoff date. "Based on DOL's own weekly allocation figure, the dismissal period should have ended on [date]. I'm appealing the denial of benefits after that date."
  • →Do NOT argue that the severance was "not much money" or "only one month." The threshold is your specific weekly benefit rate — not whether the total amount was large. The hearing officer is applying the formula, not making a fairness judgment. Argue the formula.
Partial Unemployment / Earnings Dispute
NY Labor Law §590(5) · Benefits reduced or denied for weeks with earnings
DOL says your benefits were overpaid or reduced for weeks when you also had earnings. The dispute is about what you earned, whether it was properly reported, and whether the formula was applied correctly.
Legal standard

Under §590(5), you can receive partial benefits during weeks when you have some earnings — as long as those earnings are below your weekly benefit rate (WBR). Your benefit is reduced dollar-for-dollar by earnings above your partial benefit credit (PBC).

How the partial unemployment formula works
  • 1 Partial benefit credit (PBC) 1/4 of your weekly benefit rate (rounded to nearest dollar). Earnings up to your PBC don't reduce your benefit at all. Example: WBR = $400, PBC = $100. You earn $100 or less — no reduction.
  • 2 Benefit reduction zone Earnings above your PBC reduce your benefit dollar for dollar. Example: WBR = $400, PBC = $100, you earn $250. Reduction = $250 − $100 = $150. Benefit = $400 − $150 = $250.
  • 3 Total disqualification for that week If your earnings equal or exceed your WBR, you receive $0 for that week. This affects that specific week only — it doesn't carry forward or affect other weeks in your claim.

Three things the hearing officer is testing
  • A What you actually earned during the disputed week(s) The factual dispute: DOL's records (typically from employer wage reports) vs. your documented earnings. Pay stubs, direct deposit records, and bank statements are the primary evidence sources.
  • B Whether the payment was "remuneration" Only payment for services rendered counts. Expense reimbursements, gifts, insurance payouts, and pension payments from prior employers don't count. Gig income, tips, commissions, and freelance pay do.
  • C Whether DOL applied the formula correctly Did DOL use the right WBR and PBC? Did they allocate earnings to the correct week? Was the overpayment period calculated accurately? A math error in DOL's determination is challengeable.
NOT remuneration (doesn't count against your benefit): Documented expense reimbursements (mileage, tools, supplies — must be expense-based, not additional compensation). Disability insurance benefits or workers' compensation payments. Pension or retirement payments from a former employer. A payment that is genuinely a gift with no connection to services rendered.
IS remuneration (does count and must be reported): Wages, salary, hourly pay. Cash tips and credit card tips. Commissions. Gig work income (DoorDash, Uber, Instacart, TaskRabbit, etc.). Freelance or self-employment revenue. Bonuses tied to work performed. Board or lodging provided as part of compensation.
If a false statement penalty (§597) was also added: A false statement penalty requires that you KNOWINGLY provided incorrect information. Confusion about what counts as earnings, classification disputes about gig income, or arithmetic errors are not knowing false statements. You can challenge the penalty separately even if you accept that some overpayment occurred: "I reported my earnings as I understood them. This is a classification dispute, not an intentional misstatement."
Evidence to gather before your hearing
Gross vs. net wages — the most common source of discrepancy: Employer wage records submitted to DOL report gross wages (before taxes, health insurance, retirement contributions, and other deductions). Your pay stub may show a lower net figure (what actually hit your bank account). If DOL's earnings figure is higher than yours, check whether the difference equals your payroll deductions. The figure relevant to UI calculations is gross wages. Example: you report $380 (net), employer reports $470 (gross) — the $90 difference is taxes and insurance deductions, not an error by either party. You may still need to correct the record if the wrong figure was used in the formula.
Statement guidance
  • →If DOL's earnings figure doesn't match your records, make the discrepancy explicit with numbers. "DOL alleges I earned $X during the week of [date]. My pay stub shows $Y. My bank statement shows a deposit of $Y for that week. DOL's figure appears to reflect [gross vs. net? a different pay period? a different employer?]."
  • →If the payment shouldn't count as remuneration, name the specific category. "The $X I received that week was [expense reimbursement / pension disbursement / workers' comp payment]. It was not payment for services rendered during that claim week." Then present the documentation.
  • →If challenging the formula: walk through the math out loud. "My WBR is $A. My PBC is $B (1/4 of WBR). I earned $C. The correct benefit = $A − ($C − $B) = $[result]. DOL calculated $[their result], which appears to use the wrong [WBR figure / earnings amount / pay period]."
  • →If a false statement penalty was added: address intent separately and specifically. "I reported my earnings according to my understanding of the reporting requirements. The dispute about how to classify this income does not mean I provided knowingly false information. I reported what I received in good faith."
  • →Do NOT just say "I reported everything correctly" without documents. The hearing officer will have DOL's wage records, which come from your employer independently. If your certification and the wage record differ, you need a specific explanation — not just an assertion that you were right.
Labor Dispute / Strike or Lockout
NY Labor Law §592 · Benefits suspended for weeks lost due to a labor dispute
Your benefits were denied or suspended because DOL determined your unemployment is connected to a strike, lockout, or other industrial controversy. Whether you're entitled to benefits depends on who stopped the work — and whether you were actually part of the dispute.
Legal standard

Under §592, benefits are suspended for weeks you are unemployed due to a labor dispute at your establishment — with an important exception: a lockout triggers no suspension. If the employer chose to stop employing you, you are eligible from day one.

The critical distinction: lockout vs. strike
  • 1 Strike (work stopped by employees) If employees voted to strike and walked out, a 2-week waiting period applies before benefits begin. After the 2 weeks, you are generally eligible unless you are directly participating in the continuation of the dispute.
  • 2 Lockout (work stopped by employer) If the employer locked employees out — closed the workplace, refused entry, or imposed terms that forced employees to leave — there is no suspension period. You are eligible immediately. The employer ends the work; you didn't choose to stop it.
  • 3 Not participating / not in the bargaining unit If the dispute is a strike by another union or bargaining unit, and you did not voluntarily walk out or participate, you may be eligible regardless of the work stoppage classification. The key: you did not choose to stop working.
Three things the hearing officer is testing
  • A Was the work stoppage a lockout or a strike? Who initiated the work stoppage? Employer action (notices, plant closure, refusal to let workers in, imposition of new terms) points to lockout. Employee vote and walkout points to strike. This classification determines whether any waiting period applies.
  • B Were you "directly participating" in the dispute? Did you personally walk out, honor picket lines, vote for the action, or otherwise participate? A worker who was laid off before the dispute, was on medical leave, or was in a different facility or bargaining unit may be eligible even if a strike was occurring.
  • C Has the 2-week waiting period passed? (Strike cases only) If it was a strike and more than 14 days have elapsed since the stoppage began, benefits should resume. If DOL is continuing to deny benefits past that point, the ongoing denial requires a separate basis.
Immediately eligible (no waiting period): Employer-initiated lockout. You were not employed by or in the bargaining unit involved. You were on medical or disability leave when the dispute began. You were laid off before the labor dispute started. Permanent replacement workers were hired — your position was eliminated, not paused.
Subject to 2-week suspension: You participated in a strike vote. You honored picket lines by refusing to cross. You are in the bargaining unit that went on strike. You are still "participating" in the dispute (actively honoring pickets) more than 2 weeks after the stoppage began.
Evidence to gather before your hearing
Statement guidance
  • →If it's a lockout: establish who stopped the work first. "On [date], the employer [closed the facility / posted a notice / refused entry / imposed new terms]. Workers did not choose to stop working — the employer stopped the work. This is a lockout under §592, which does not create a waiting period."
  • →If you weren't participating: be explicit about your relationship to the dispute. "I am [not a member of the bargaining unit / employed at a different facility / not a member of the union that called the strike]. I did not participate in the strike vote. I did not honor picket lines. My unemployment is not a result of participation in the dispute — it's a result of [the facility closing / my position being affected by circumstances outside the dispute]."
  • →If the 2-week period has passed: make that timeline explicit. "The work stoppage began on [date]. Two weeks from that date is [date]. My benefits should have been restored on [date]. DOL's continued denial after that date requires a separate legal basis that has not been established."
  • →If your prior employment status matters: establish it chronologically. "I was [on approved FMLA leave / laid off from the position / at the out-of-state facility] at the time the dispute began on [date]. The dispute did not cause my unemployment — [the medical leave / the layoff] did."
  • →Do NOT argue about whether the strike was justified. Whether the employees had good reasons to strike is not the legal question. The questions are: who stopped the work, were you participating, and has the waiting period expired. Keep your statement focused on those elements.
About the hearing

Format: NY UI hearings are typically 45–90 minutes, conducted by phone or video (the VHC platform). An Administrative Law Judge (ALJ) opens by identifying the issues, both sides testify and may present documents, and cross-examination is allowed. You can request a free transcript if you need to appeal further.

Representation: You may represent yourself or bring a representative (attorney, union rep, legal aid advocate). Legal aid organizations in NY offer free hearing representation for eligible claimants. LawNY and Legal Services NYC are starting points.

If the ALJ rules against you: You can appeal to the Unemployment Insurance Appeal Board (UIAB) — typically within 20 days of the ALJ decision. File your UIAB appeal at uiappeals.labor.ny.gov. The UIAB reviews the hearing record and can affirm, reverse, or remand. If the UIAB affirms against you, you may petition the Appellate Division (an Article 78 proceeding in state Supreme Court). Each step has its own hard deadline printed on the decision letter — read it immediately and count the days from the date on the letter, not from when you receive it.
