Credit Report Rating Codes: R1–R9, I1–I9, M1–M9, O1–O9 Explained

Every credit account on your report gets a two-character code: a letter (account type) and a digit (payment status). Here is what every combination means, how long each stays, and what its scoring impact is.

How to find these codes: Download your free report from AnnualCreditReport.com (the only federally mandated free source). Rating codes appear in the "Account Status," "Payment Pattern," or "Account History" section of each tradeline. Not all bureaus display them in the same column, but all three major bureaus (Equifax, Experian, TransUnion) use this coding system.

What the Letter Means: Account Type

R

Revolving

Credit cards, store cards, home equity lines of credit (HELOCs), and other accounts with a credit limit and a varying monthly balance. You can borrow up to the limit repeatedly as you pay down the balance.

I

Installment

Loans with fixed monthly payments and a defined payoff date: auto loans, student loans, personal loans, boat loans. The loan amount is fixed at origination and decreases with each payment.

M

Mortgage

Home purchase mortgages, home equity loans (HELoan — fixed amount, fixed payments, not a line of credit). Some reports include manufactured home loans here. Distinguished from HELOCs, which are revolving (R).

O

Open / 30-Day

Charge cards (American Express Green/Gold — must be paid in full each month), certain utility accounts, and accounts expected to be fully paid monthly. Rare on modern reports; most "open" cards are coded R.

What the Number Means: Payment Status

The digit (0–9) describes the current or worst-ever payment history on the account. A credit report may show both the current status and a historical worst-status code depending on the bureau's format.

Digit Meaning Score Impact Notes
0 Too new to rate — account exists but not enough history to assign a payment status Neutral Disappears once 6 months of history exists. Normal for new accounts.
1 Paid as agreed — always on time, no derogatory history Positive The best possible status. Accounts with a long R1 or I1 history are the backbone of a high credit score.
2 30–59 days past due — at least one payment 30 to 59 days late Minor negative A single 30-day late payment can drop a score 50–100 points depending on the starting score and account age. Stays 7 years from the late payment date.
3 60–89 days past due — payment 60 to 89 days late Major negative Significantly more damaging than a 30-day late. Most lenders view 60-day lates as a serious delinquency. Stays 7 years.
4 90–119 days past due — payment 90 to 119 days late Major negative At this stage many creditors assign the account to internal collections and begin the process leading to charge-off. Stays 7 years.
5 120+ days past due — payment 120 or more days late Severe One step from charge-off. The account is typically flagged for write-off. Lenders rarely extend credit while a 5-status account is open. Stays 7 years.
6 Making regular payments under an arrangement — debt management plan, consolidation arrangement, or creditor workout plan Negative Shows the creditor agreed to modified terms — this signals prior distress. Often seen when a consumer uses a nonprofit credit counseling agency. Not as damaging as 8 or 9 but negative. Stays 7 years from the arrangement date.
7 Paying through Chapter 13 bankruptcy — the account is included in an active wage-earner repayment plan Severe Chapter 13 involves a court-supervised repayment plan (3–5 years). The account shows 7-status while the plan is active. Chapter 13 filing stays on the report for 7 years from the filing date.
8 Repossession or foreclosure — the lender took the collateral Severe For installment accounts (I8): vehicle repossession, boat/equipment repossession. For mortgages (M8): foreclosure. For revolving accounts (R8): rare but occasionally used for certain secured cards where the collateral was seized. Stays 7 years from first delinquency.
9 Seriously delinquent — charged off, written off as bad debt, sent to collections, or included in Chapter 7 bankruptcy Most severe The worst single-account rating. R9 is a credit card charged off or in collections. I9 is a loan charged off. M9 is a mortgage in foreclosure proceedings (before the lender takes the property) or written off. Stays 7 years from the date of first delinquency — not from the charge-off date.

How Long Each Code Stays on Your Report

Codes Removal Timeline Clock Starts From
X0 Disappears on its own — typically within 6 months once payment history builds N/A
X1 Stays indefinitely while account is open and in good standing; typically 10 years after closing N/A (positive accounts may remain)
X2 – X5 (late payments) 7 years Date of the specific late payment
X6 (arrangement) 7 years Date arrangement began or date of first delinquency leading to it
X7 (Chapter 13) 7 years Filing date of the Chapter 13 bankruptcy
X8 (repossession / foreclosure) 7 years Date of first delinquency that led to the repossession or foreclosure — not the date the vehicle/property was taken
X9 (charge-off / collections) 7 years Date of first delinquency — not the date of charge-off, not the date sent to collections, not when a new collector buys the debt. This is the most commonly misunderstood rule: a debt collector cannot reset the 7-year clock by re-reporting the account as a new collection.
The clock rule that matters most: For X9 (charge-off) and X8 (repossession/foreclosure), the 7-year removal clock starts from the date of first delinquency — the first missed payment that led to the eventual charge-off. It does not restart when the account is sold to a collection agency. If a collector re-reports your charged-off debt as a new collection with a recent date, this may be a violation of the Fair Credit Reporting Act. You can dispute it with the bureau.

Three Things Paying Off a Charged-Off Account Will NOT Do

This is the most common misunderstanding about X9 codes:

1. It will not remove the R9/I9/M9 code. Paying a charged-off account settles the debt but does not erase the derogatory mark. The account will update to show a $0 balance with a note like "Paid charge-off" but the original charge-off history and the R9/I9 notation remain for the full 7-year period.

2. It will not stop the 7-year clock. The removal date was set when the first payment was missed. Payment — whether voluntary or after a lawsuit — does not change that date.

3. It will not prevent a collection account from also appearing. When a charged-off account is sold to a collection agency, a new tradeline from the collector appears separately. You may see both the original creditor's R9 and the collection agency's account. Both count against you. Paying the collector does not remove the original creditor's R9.

The exception: Pay-for-delete. Some collection agencies agree in writing to remove their tradeline entirely upon payment. This is not required by law and is at the collector's discretion. If a collector offers this, get it in writing before paying. It affects only the collection tradeline — the original creditor's charge-off remains.

How to Dispute an Incorrect Rating Code

If you believe a rating code on your report is wrong — wrong status, wrong dates, wrong account — you have the right under the Fair Credit Reporting Act (FCRA) to dispute it at no cost.

What can be disputed: Factual errors only — an R9 on an account you never missed a payment on; an I8 repo on a loan you paid off; a late payment date that is wrong; an account that is not yours. Accurate negative information cannot be forced off early by disputing.

How to file: Submit disputes directly to each bureau that shows the error: Equifax (equifax.com/personal/credit-report-services/), Experian (experian.com/disputes/), TransUnion (transunion.com/credit-disputes/). Include supporting documentation — payment records, account statements, bank confirmation numbers.

Timeline: Bureaus have 30 days to investigate (45 days if you provided additional documentation) and must notify you of the result. If they cannot verify the information, they must remove or correct it.

Frequently Asked Questions

What does R9 mean on a credit report?
R9 means a revolving account (credit card or line of credit) that is seriously delinquent — typically charged off, in collections, or written off as a bad debt. The "R" identifies it as revolving credit and the "9" is the worst-possible status digit. An R9 stays on your credit report for 7 years from the date of first delinquency and is one of the most damaging entries possible.
What does R1 mean?
R1 means a revolving account paid as agreed — always on time, no late payments. R1 is the best possible rating for a revolving account and actively benefits your credit score over time.
What does I9 mean?
I9 means an installment account (auto loan, student loan, personal loan) that is seriously delinquent — charged off or written off. Same meaning as R9 but for a loan rather than revolving credit.
What does M8 mean?
M8 means a mortgage account where the lender took back the property — a foreclosure. This is one of the most damaging marks on a credit report and stays for 7 years from the date of first delinquency.
What does R7 mean?
R7 means a revolving account being paid through a Chapter 13 bankruptcy (court-supervised repayment plan). The account is included in the plan and payments are being made through the bankruptcy trustee. Chapter 13 stays on the report for 7 years from the filing date.
Does paying a charged-off account (R9) improve my score?
Possibly slightly, but far less than most people expect. A paid R9 updates to show $0 balance but the charge-off notation and the delinquency history remain. FICO Score 9 and VantageScore 3.0+ weigh paid collections less heavily than unpaid ones, so there can be a modest improvement — but the account stays on your report for the full 7-year period regardless. The main benefit of paying is to avoid potential lawsuits (creditors can sue during the statute of limitations, which is separate from the 7-year credit reporting rule) and to clear the debt legally.
Can a collection agency re-age my debt to make it last longer?
No. This practice — called "re-aging" — is illegal under the FCRA. The 7-year clock is set from the date of first delinquency with the original creditor, regardless of how many times the debt changes hands. A new collection agency that purchases old debt cannot report it as a new account with a new delinquency date. If a collector does this, you can dispute it with the bureaus and may have grounds for an FCRA complaint with the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB).
