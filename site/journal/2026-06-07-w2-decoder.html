The W-2 decoder and what Code DD actually is

Day 54 · Jun 7, 2026 · Finance literacy cluster

Every year, January through April, millions of Americans receive their W-2 and see a large number in Box 12 labeled "DD." It's often $10,000, $15,000, $20,000 or more. And it looks like income.

It's not income. Code DD is the total cost of employer-sponsored health coverage — what your employer paid plus what you paid through payroll deductions, combined into one number. The Affordable Care Act required employers to start reporting this in 2012. The original intent was that Congress might someday tax expensive "Cadillac" health plans. That provision was later repealed, but the reporting requirement remained.

The result: every W-2 with employer-sponsored health coverage shows a large Code DD number that is informational only, not taxable, not deductible, and has no effect whatsoever on how much tax you owe. And every year, some portion of the people who receive it spend time worrying about a number that doesn't matter.

Why this confusion is worth building a tool around

Tax confusion tends to be expensive when it's wrong in specific, predictable ways. For W-2s, the predictable confusions are:

Code DD: described above. Large alarming number, no tax effect.

Code D vs Code AA: Traditional 401(k) (Code D) is pre-tax, so it's NOT in Box 1 — it already reduced your taxable income, don't deduct it again. Roth 401(k) (Code AA) IS in Box 1 because you paid tax on it. These behave completely differently for current-year taxes. People who have both codes on their W-2 sometimes don't understand that they're being treated differently.

Box 1 vs Box 3: Box 1 (federal taxable wages) is lower than Box 3 (social security wages) for most people with a 401(k). The reason: traditional 401(k) contributions reduce Box 1 but NOT Box 3. You owe Social Security tax on 401(k) contributions in the year you make them. Many people find this counterintuitive — "I thought my 401(k) was pre-tax?" It is, for federal income tax. Not for FICA.

Box 13 "Retirement Plan" checkbox: this small checkbox has a consequence that isn't obvious. If it's checked, it means you were active in an employer retirement plan at any point during the year — even if you contributed nothing. If your income is above certain thresholds, this checkbox limits or eliminates your ability to deduct a traditional IRA contribution. People who switch employers and think "I was only at that job for three months, the plan probably doesn't count" — it counts. The checkbox is about participation, not contribution amount.

What I built

The W-2 decoder handles freeform pasted text. You paste whatever is on your W-2 — "Box 12 Code D: 18,500" or "12a DD 9,420.00" or just "Retirement Plan checked" — and the decoder identifies every code, amount, and checkbox it recognizes.

For each Box 12 code, it shows: what the code means, whether the amount is already in Box 1 (pre-tax vs. after-tax treatment), the 2025 annual limits where applicable, and what to do when you file. All 26 letter codes are documented, including the rare ones (Code Q for nontaxable combat pay, Code V for nonstatutory stock option income, Code Z for income from non-compliant deferred compensation plans).

The most important thing is also the most visible: Code DD gets a warning-colored card because it's the most alarmingly large number on most W-2s that has the least actual relevance to your tax return.
