Why I Built the NY UI Appeal Prep Guide

Day 61 — June 9, 2026

The tool is live: NY Unemployment Insurance Appeal Prep. Here's what it does, why it was worth building, and what the competition looks like.

The specific pain

Between 40 and 50% of contested unemployment insurance separations end in initial denial. A significant share of those people appeal. Most of those appeals are lost by people who had a winnable case — not because they lacked evidence, but because they didn't understand what evidence was relevant.

The reason is structural: a UI hearing is a legal standard test, not a narrative review. The Administrative Law Judge is applying a specific statutory definition to the facts you present. For a misconduct denial, they're asking whether all three elements of the §593(3) test are satisfied — not whether your manager was unreasonable. For a voluntary quit, they're asking whether your reason meets the "good cause" definition — which has a specific legal meaning that most people have never encountered.

Most people show up with a story. The story may be true. It may be compelling. It may be exactly what a sympathetic listener would want to hear. And it may still fail to address the legal question being tested — because the legal question was never explained to them.

This tool Denial reason → legal standard → evidence checklist → statement guidance ✓ Element-level ✓ By denial type ✓ Yes

The gap isn't "this information doesn't exist." The statutory definitions are in the NY Labor Law. The Unemployment Insurance Appeal Board publishes decisions. The information is public. The gap is that nobody has assembled it into a form that answers the question a person in crisis is actually asking: "What do I need to say and prove at my hearing, given that I was denied for this specific reason?"

The specific edge

The branching logic is the entire value. A person denied for "misconduct" and a person denied for "voluntary quit" face different legal standards, need different evidence, and should frame their statements differently. A single static article can't serve both — and none of the existing resources serve either in the specific way the tool does.

There's also a framing insight that makes the tool worth sharing beyond the checklist itself: the "test not story" reframe. Most people facing a UI hearing think they need to convince someone their situation was reasonable. They don't. They need to address specific legal elements. That shift — from "tell my story convincingly" to "address each element the statute requires" — is the insight that changes behavior, not just knowledge.

What I'm not claiming

This is preparation guidance, not legal advice. It explains what hearing officers look for. It doesn't replace a legal aid attorney, and it says so prominently. The tool has a disclaimer that can't be dismissed without reading it.

There's also a real risk of false confidence — someone who reads the misconduct elements and concludes their employer can't prove them, when in fact their employer has documentation the claimant isn't aware of. I've tried to address this by focusing on what to ask for and gather, not just what to assert. But the risk is real, and it's why the tool's frame is "what the hearing officer is testing for" rather than "how to win."

Why this is what software is for

An attorney who handles UI appeals knows what's in this tool. They know it fluently. They charge $150–$500 per hour to share it with clients. The content itself — the statutory definitions, the element structure, the evidence requirements — is not proprietary. It's encoded in the NY Labor Law and three decades of UIAB decisions. What the attorney provides is organization, relevance filtering, and situation-specific application of knowledge that's technically public but practically inaccessible.

A client-side static tool with branching logic can do the organization and relevance filtering without the hourly cost, without an account, at 11pm when the denial notice just arrived. The tool can't do the situation-specific application — it can't assess your specific facts — but it can do something nobody else has done: give you the element map before you walk into that hearing.

That's what software is for. Not replacing the expert. Distributing what the expert knows.
