Working While Claiming: Why the Formula Matters

Partial unemployment is one of the most widely misunderstood parts of New York's UI system. People either don't know they can work part-time while claiming, report incorrectly because they don't understand what to report, or find themselves facing an overpayment determination with a penalty on top. All three problems come from the same source: the formula is never explained.

If you currently have a pending appeal involving earnings — DOL says you were overpaid, or that your benefits were incorrectly reduced — this is what you need to know before your hearing.

The formula itself

Under NY Labor Law §590(5), you can receive reduced benefits during weeks when you have some earnings. The formula:

Benefit = WBR − (Earnings − PBC) Where: WBR = your weekly benefit rate (from your monetary determination letter) PBC = partial benefit credit = WBR ÷ 4 (rounded to the nearest dollar) Example: WBR = $400, PBC = $100 Earnings = $150 → Benefit = $400 − ($150 − $100) = $400 − $50 = $350 Earnings = $250 → Benefit = $400 − ($250 − $100) = $400 − $150 = $250 Earnings = $400 → $0 benefit for that week (earnings ≥ WBR)

The partial benefit credit is the zone where earnings don't affect your benefit at all. If you earn less than your PBC, your benefit is unchanged. If you earn more, the excess reduces your benefit dollar-for-dollar. If you earn more than your WBR, you get nothing that week — but only that week. The disqualification doesn't carry over.

What counts as earnings (and what doesn't)

This is where most disputes begin. DOL broadly interprets "remuneration" — any payment for services rendered counts, regardless of how it's labeled or when it's paid:

These count and must be reported: hourly wages, salary, cash tips, credit card tips, commissions, gig work income (DoorDash, Instacart, TaskRabbit, Uber, Lyft), freelance or self-employment revenue, bonuses tied to work performed, and the fair market value of board or lodging provided as compensation.

These do not count: documented expense reimbursements (mileage, tools, supplies — must be expense-based, not additional compensation), disability insurance benefits, workers' compensation payments, pension or retirement distributions from a former employer, and payments that are genuinely gifts with no connection to services rendered.

The gig economy category is where the most confusion lives. When you drive for DoorDash, the platform reports gross revenue to the IRS — not what you "netted" after expenses. For UI purposes, earnings are allocated to the week the work was performed, not the week you were paid. That matters if you receive a weekly payment on Fridays for work done the prior week.

The gross vs. net problem

The single most common source of mismatch between what a claimant reports and what DOL records show is the gross vs. net distinction. Your employer reports your wages to DOL using gross wages — the figure before taxes, health insurance premiums, 401(k) contributions, and other deductions are taken out. Your pay stub shows net wages — what actually hit your bank account.

If you reported your net wage, and your employer reported your gross wage, both numbers are "correct" — they just represent different things. This doesn't mean DOL's number is right for purposes of the formula. The correct figure for UI calculations is gross wages. But if you received an overpayment determination and the discrepancy between your certification and DOL's wage record equals your payroll deductions, that's the explanation.

At a hearing, you'd show: your pay stub (which shows both gross and net), your bank statement (which shows the net deposit), and then walk through the arithmetic: "I reported $380. The employer reported $470. The difference of $90 equals my tax and insurance deductions for that week. I was reporting my take-home pay. The gross figure is $470."

The false statement penalty

This is what transforms a run-of-the-mill overpayment dispute into something genuinely serious. Under NY Labor Law §597, if DOL determines you knowingly provided false information to obtain benefits, they can impose a penalty of up to 15 weeks of benefits — on top of the overpayment repayment. That means repaying what you received, plus forfeiting 15 future weeks of eligibility.

The word "knowingly" is load-bearing here. A classification dispute, an honest mistake about what counts as earnings, or a misunderstanding about the gross/net distinction are not knowing false statements. Confusion is not fraud. The §597 penalty requires intent — DOL must show you knew the statement was false when you made it.

If a §597 penalty was added to your determination: Challenge the penalty separately, even if you acknowledge some overpayment occurred. "I reported what I received as I understood the reporting requirement. I did not knowingly provide false information. This is a classification dispute, not an intentional misstatement." The penalty requires intent — and DOL bears the burden of showing you had it.

What to bring to your hearing

If you're appealing a partial unemployment overpayment, your evidence set is: your weekly certifications (print or screenshot exactly what you submitted), pay stubs for the disputed weeks (showing gross and net), bank statements for those periods, and DOL's overpayment determination letter with their exact numbers. You cannot challenge a calculation you haven't read — the determination letter contains DOL's earnings figure, their WBR and PBC figures, and the formula they applied. Get all of that before your hearing.

Then run the formula yourself. If DOL's math is wrong, identify exactly where: wrong WBR figure, wrong earnings amount, wrong pay period allocation, or wrong formula. "The correct benefit for that week was X, not Y, because..." is a much more effective hearing presentation than "I reported my earnings correctly."
