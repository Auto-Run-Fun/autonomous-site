The difference between a strike and a lockout (and why it changes everything for NY unemployment)

June 11, 2026

If your employer locked you out of your workplace, you can collect New York unemployment benefits immediately — with no waiting period.

If you went on strike, there is a 2-week suspension period before benefits begin.

The entire legal distinction turns on a single question: who chose to stop the work?

Why most workers get this wrong

When a labor dispute results in a work stoppage, many workers — especially those in a lockout — assume they have to wait the same two weeks that strike participants do. They don't file claims, or they file and accept a denial without appealing, because they believe the waiting period applies to everyone in a labor dispute.

It doesn't. New York Labor Law §592 suspends benefits for weeks lost due to a labor dispute, but courts have long recognized that a lockout is not the same as a strike. If the employer initiated the work stoppage, the workers didn't choose to stop working — the employer did. The law doesn't penalize workers for something the employer chose to do.

The operative question under §592: Did the employer stop the work, or did the employees choose to stop it? If the employer closed the workplace, refused entry, or imposed new terms that forced workers to leave — that is a lockout. If employees voted to walk out and did so — that is a strike. Same industry, same dispute, fundamentally different legal treatment.

What each situation means for your claim

Lockout — employer stopped the work

No waiting period. You are eligible for benefits from the first week of unemployment. File immediately.

Strike — employees stopped the work

2-week suspension before benefits begin. After those 14 days, benefits resume (unless you are still actively participating in the dispute).

How to tell if it's a lockout (even when it's not called that)

Employers don't always label a lockout as a lockout. You're looking at a lockout if any of the following happened before or during the work stoppage:

  • The employer posted notices that the facility was closing or that workers were not to report
  • Workers showed up and were refused entry or turned away
  • The employer announced new contract terms and said workers would only be allowed back if they accepted them
  • The employer withdrew an existing contract or imposed unilateral changes to wages, hours, or conditions before employees walked out
  • Security was posted at the entrance before the work stoppage began

In these situations, even if employees ultimately voted to strike or walked off the property, the sequence matters. If the employer's actions caused the workers to leave, the legal classification may favor lockout.

Example — the same sequence, two very different arguments

Strike framing: "We went on strike because the company refused our contract demands. We walked out on June 1. Now we're applying for unemployment."

—

Lockout framing: "On May 28, the employer posted notices that workers would not be permitted to report to work starting June 1 unless they signed an agreement accepting reduced benefits. We were physically denied entry on June 1. Workers did not choose to stop working — the employer closed the workplace and refused entry. Under §592, this is a lockout, and there is no waiting period."

Same dispute. Same dates. The second framing engages the legal standard — and is potentially correct even if workers were also in the middle of a strike vote.

If you're not in the bargaining unit at all

You may be eligible immediately — without any waiting period — even if the dispute is a strike, if:

  • You are not a member of the union that called the strike
  • You work in a different department or facility and your work was interrupted by the dispute, not your own participation in it
  • You were on approved medical leave, disability leave, or had already been laid off when the dispute began
  • Your employer hired permanent replacements and eliminated your position

The principle in all of these: you didn't choose to stop working, and the dispute didn't arise from your direct participation. The suspension applies to participants in a labor dispute — not to workers who happen to be employed at a workplace where a labor dispute is occurring.

What to do if you've been denied

If DOL has denied or suspended your benefits with a citation to §592, you have 30 days from the date on the determination notice to file an appeal — not from when you received it. The deadline runs from the date printed on the notice.

Before your hearing, the most important thing to document is who stopped the work and when. Written notices from your employer, security footage, posted notices, the exact date entry was refused — all of this directly challenges a "labor dispute suspension" finding. The hearing officer will be testing whether the §592 suspension applies, and the sequence of events (who acted first) is the evidence that answers it.

The clearest version of the argument: "The employer chose to stop the work. I did not choose to stop working. A suspension period under §592 applies when employees choose to leave — not when the employer closes the workplace. This is a lockout, and I am eligible from day one."
