What Happens If You Don't Appeal Your Unemployment Denial?

The short answer: the denial becomes final. You forfeit all benefits for that claim period — permanently. And most denials can be reversed with the right preparation.

If you just received a NY unemployment denial notice and you're not sure whether to appeal, this is what you need to know before the deadline passes.

What you lose if you don't appeal

When you don't appeal a UI denial in New York, the determination becomes final. That means:

You lose all benefits tied to that claim. Not just the week you were denied — the entire benefit period. If you were denied for separation from your last employer, a non-appeal means you cannot collect unemployment insurance for that job loss at all, regardless of how long you were employed or how much you contributed to the system.

The record stands permanently. A denial that goes unappealed becomes the official determination of your eligibility. You cannot re-open it later because you found a better argument or a document you missed. The hearing is your one chance to present your case.

If you're appealing a disqualification (not just a week), the stakes are larger. Many denials cover the full benefit year. A misconduct denial, a voluntary quit denial, or a refusal-of-suitable-work denial typically disqualifies you from the entire remaining benefit period — not one week. Not appealing means accepting that entire disqualification without challenge.

What appealing actually costs

Nothing, in money. The New York UI appeal process is free and does not require an attorney.

Filing the appeal: Online at labor.ny.gov or by mail to the ALJ office on your denial notice. Takes 15 minutes.

The hearing: A phone call with an Administrative Law Judge (ALJ). Usually 30–60 minutes. You present your side. The employer (if involved) presents theirs. The ALJ asks questions and applies the legal standard to the facts.

The outcome: The ALJ issues a written decision, typically within 4–8 weeks of the hearing. About 40% of appealed cases are reversed or modified.

If you lose at the ALJ level: You can appeal to the Unemployment Insurance Appeals Board (UIAB) within 20 days. After that, Article 78 review in Appellate Division.

You do not need a lawyer. You do not pay a filing fee. The hearing is conducted by phone — you don't go to a courthouse. The only thing appealing costs is the time to prepare.

The deadline is harder than you think

30 days from the date on the notice — not from when you received it. Mail delays are common. If your denial notice is dated June 1 and arrived June 10, your deadline is July 1 — not July 10. If you received your notice more than 20 days after the date printed on it, file your appeal immediately. Do not wait.

The 30-day deadline in New York is a hard cutoff. Missing it almost always means you cannot appeal, regardless of the reason. There are narrow exceptions for "good cause" but they are rarely granted. If you are close to the deadline, file now and prepare after.

Whether to appeal

Appeal if any of these are true:

You believe the denial reason is wrong on the facts — you didn't engage in misconduct, you didn't voluntarily quit without cause, you were available and able to work. You have documents or a witness account that contradicts what the employer reported. You don't fully understand why you were denied (the denial notice may be terse and the legal standard is not obvious). You were denied for a technical reason — earnings dispute, severance pay calculation, part-time work during your claim — and you believe the calculation is incorrect.

Don't not appeal simply because you're not sure you'll win. The standard for filing an appeal is lower than the standard for winning one. Filing is free and preserves your options. Not filing closes them permanently.

What the hearing is actually testing

Most people lose hearings they could have won — not because they lacked evidence, but because they didn't know what the hearing officer was testing for. A misconduct denial isn't asking "did you do the thing." It's asking whether what you did meets the specific three-element legal definition of misconduct under NY Labor Law §593(3). A voluntary quit denial isn't asking "why did you leave." It's asking whether your reason meets the legal standard for "good cause."

Preparation that doesn't account for the actual legal standard being applied is preparation aimed at the wrong target.
