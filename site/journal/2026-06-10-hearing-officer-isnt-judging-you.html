The hearing officer isn't judging you

June 10, 2026

You received a denial notice. You have 14 days to file an appeal. You're thinking about what you'll say at the hearing — how to explain what happened, how to make it clear you didn't deserve to be fired (or that your reason for leaving was valid), how to come across as honest and reasonable.

Stop. That preparation is wrong. Not because your story isn't true. It is wrong because the hearing officer isn't listening for what you think they're listening for.

What the hearing officer is actually doing

A UI hearing is a statutory test. The Administrative Law Judge is applying a specific section of New York Labor Law to the facts in front of them. They are not evaluating whether you were treated fairly. They are not deciding whether your manager was a reasonable person. They are checking whether the facts you present satisfy — or fail to satisfy — the legal elements of the relevant test.

For a misconduct denial, the test is a three-part question under §593(3): Was the conduct connected to employment? Was it willful or deliberate? Did it show disregard for the employer's legitimate interests? The employer must prove all three. If any one is missing, the denial doesn't hold — regardless of how sympathetic or unsympathetic the story sounds.

For a voluntary quit, the test under §593(1) is whether you had "good cause" — which has a specific legal meaning. It doesn't mean "a good reason." It means circumstances that developed in your employment that would have justified refusing the job from the start, combined with evidence that you tried to address the problem before leaving.

Most people don't know this. They prepare by thinking about their story. The story is true. It may be moving. And it may completely fail to address the question being asked.

The shift that changes your preparation

Once you understand the hearing is a legal standard test, your preparation changes completely.

Instead of thinking: "How do I explain what happened so they understand my side?"

You think: "Which elements of the test is my employer likely to argue they've proven? Which elements are actually weak? What evidence refutes those elements specifically?"

Misconduct denial — the difference in practice

Wrong preparation: "I'll explain that I only missed three days, that my manager was unfair, and that I had a good record for four years. I'll be honest and reasonable and they'll see I didn't deserve to be fired."

—

Right preparation: "My employer needs to prove all three elements of §593(3). Element 1 (connected to employment) — they can probably establish that. Element 2 (willful or deliberate) — my absences were due to a documented illness; inability is different from unwillingness. Element 3 (disregard for interests) — I have a four-year clean record; a single incident after four years doesn't demonstrate disregard. I'll lead with element 2 and support it with my doctor's documentation. I'll introduce element 3 using my performance history."

Same situation. Same facts. Completely different hearing.

What to do right now

First, read your denial notice carefully. The denial reason is usually in the first paragraph. It will say something like "discharged for misconduct" or "left without good cause" or "not available for work." That phrase tells you which statutory test applies.

Second, find out what the hearing officer will be testing. For a misconduct denial under §593(3), they're testing the three-element test described above. For a voluntary quit under §593(1), they're testing whether your reason meets the "good cause" definition. The statute language is the map.

Third, gather evidence that speaks to the elements — not evidence that supports your general story. "I have emails showing my manager was hostile to me" is only useful if it's relevant to an element of the test. "I have emails showing I submitted a written accommodation request and it was denied" addresses element 2 of the voluntary quit test directly.

The most important thing you can do in the next 14 days: identify which specific element or elements of the applicable test your employer will struggle to prove — and prepare evidence and language that directly addresses those elements. Everything else is noise.

One more thing about the hearing itself

The hearing officer will open by asking you to tell your side. This is not an invitation to narrate from the beginning. It is an opportunity to address the legal standard. Start with the element. "I want to address whether my conduct was willful and whether it showed disregard for the company's interests. Here's what the record shows on each of those questions."

The employer will present evidence. You have the right to cross-examine their witnesses. "Is this policy documented in the employee handbook I was given?" "Did you personally witness the incident?" These are not aggressive questions — they are the exact questions that test the elements the employer must prove.

You're not there to win an argument. You're there to demonstrate that the legal standard isn't met. Those are different things, and the difference shows up in what you say, what you bring, and how you open.
